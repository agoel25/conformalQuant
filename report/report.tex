\begin{abstract}
Prediction intervals for financial returns are a core risk-management deliverable, yet the industry-standard GARCH and Filtered Historical Simulation (FHS) methods rely on distributional assumptions that break during crises. Conformal prediction (CP) offers a distribution-free alternative but its finite-sample validity assumes exchangeable data, violated by autocorrelated and regime-shifting returns. We evaluate six conformal methods (Split, ACI, EnbPI, AgACI, TCP, TCP-RM) against FHS and GARCH on daily log returns across nine (ticker, crisis) pairs spanning the 2008 financial crisis, the 2020 COVID crash, and the 2022 rate-hike cycle, using five base predictors (linear AR, histogram-binning, gradient-boosted trees, LSTM, Transformer). On a combined calibration-sharpness score (Winkler), TCP and TCP-RM are the only methods that match nominal 90\% marginal coverage on average, the only methods within $0.10$ of the diagonal in conditional-coverage space, and recover coverage in $\sim 17$ trading days after the 2008 Lehman onset versus $109$--$137$ days for every other method tested. Coverage is robust to the choice of base predictor (LSTM, Transformer, simple/tree models all give similar TCP coverage), indicating that the rolling-window calibration is the operative mechanism rather than the predictor's sharpness.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

A one-day return forecast is operationally useless without an accompanying uncertainty interval: risk desks, margin calculations, and regulatory capital all depend on $(1-\alpha)$-coverage bands around the point estimate. The financial industry handles this with parametric volatility models such as GARCH \citep{engle1982,bollerslev1986} and semi-parametric wrappers like Filtered Historical Simulation (FHS) \citep{barone2001}, both of which are standard in practice and enshrined in risk regulation. Both assume a fixed innovation distribution (often conditional normal or Student-$t$) and can systematically understate tail risk during crises \citep{fantazzini2024}.

Conformal prediction (CP) \citep{vovk2005} is a distribution-free framework that builds intervals from the empirical distribution of past residuals, with finite-sample coverage guarantees under data exchangeability. Financial returns violate exchangeability --- they are autocorrelated, exhibit volatility clustering, and shift abruptly during crises --- so the question is not whether CP's guarantees hold formally, but whether CP methods produce empirically useful intervals. Several CP variants have been designed for sequential, non-stationary data: \citet{gibbs2021} introduce Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) which updates the miscoverage target online; \citet{xu2021} propose EnbPI, using leave-one-out residuals from bootstrapped ensembles; \citet{zaffran2022} propose AgACI, running ACI with many learning rates in parallel; and \citet{aich2026} recently introduced Temporal Conformal Prediction (TCP), a rolling-window split-conformal variant with an optional Robbins--Monro offset.

No prior work has compared this full family against industry baselines across multiple crisis regimes, with neural base predictors, using a primary adaptation-speed metric. We fill that gap. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
    \item A systematic comparison of six CP methods and two industry baselines across $5$ base predictors, $9$ (ticker, crisis) pairs, and five metrics (marginal coverage, conditional coverage by volatility regime, interval width, Winkler score \citep{gneiting2007}, and adaptation speed).
    \item An extension of TCP to neural base predictors (LSTM, Transformer). \citet{aich2026} evaluate only gradient-boosted tree quantile forecasters on a single COVID-window; we show their rolling-window design transfers cleanly to neural predictors and evaluate its coverage trajectory across three structurally distinct crises (2008 Lehman, 2020 COVID, 2022 rate cycle).
    \item Adaptation speed formalized as a primary diagnostic: days until rolling $20$-day coverage returns to $0.85$ for five consecutive days after the exogenous crisis onset. This metric directly captures the risk-management question of how long a miscalibrated interval persists.
\end{enumerate}

Our headline finding is that TCP and TCP-RM are the only methods that simultaneously (i) match nominal $0.90$ marginal coverage on average, (ii) keep the low-vol/high-vol coverage gap at $\leq 0.09$, and (iii) recover coverage within $\sim 17$ days of a crisis onset, versus $\geq 109$ days for every non-TCP method during the 2008 window. This advantage holds across both neural base predictors we paired with TCP (LSTM and Transformer give Winkler scores within $0.013$ of each other), suggesting the rolling-window calibration is the operative mechanism rather than any specific predictor's sharpness.


\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Industry volatility models.} GARCH \citep{engle1982,bollerslev1986} parameterizes conditional variance as an autoregression on past squared innovations and past variance, enabling one-step volatility forecasts and Gaussian-\ or Student-$t$--based intervals. FHS \citep{barone2001} replaces the distributional assumption with bootstrap sampling over standardized GARCH residuals. Both methods underperform when tail-event frequencies deviate from the fitted distribution or when parameters drift.

\textbf{Classical conformal prediction.} \citet{vovk2005} establish finite-sample marginal coverage under exchangeability via the quantile of nonconformity scores. Split conformal prediction (the variant we use as a baseline) trains the predictor on one data slice, calibrates on a disjoint slice, and uses the calibration-quantile as a symmetric half-width.

\textbf{Time-series conformal prediction.} \citet{gibbs2021} relax exchangeability by updating the target miscoverage $\alpha_t$ online from observed coverage errors (ACI). \citet{xu2021} avoid the train/calibration split by using leave-one-out residuals from an ensemble of bootstrap-trained predictors (EnbPI), with residuals maintained in a sliding window. \citet{zaffran2022} aggregate multiple ACI instances with different learning rates via an online-expert algorithm (AgACI), removing the need to tune the learning rate. Most recently, \citet{aich2026} propose TCP: rolling split-conformal on a 252-day window, with an optional Robbins--Monro offset on the conformal threshold (TCP-RM). Our work is a direct extension of theirs --- we benchmark against the broader CP family, extend TCP to neural base predictors (their experiments use gradient-boosted trees only), and evaluate across three distinct crises rather than a single COVID window.

\textbf{Finance-specific comparisons.} \citet{fantazzini2024} compares ACI against GARCH on $4{,}000$ crypto-assets for VaR estimation, finding ACI favorable but with a narrow methodological scope (one CP method, tail risk only). Our evaluation is broader (six CP methods, full interval quality, multi-regime equities) and uses exogenous crisis dates, not data-derived onsets.


\section{Methods}

\subsection{Setup}

We work on daily log-returns $r_t = \log(P_t / P_{t-1})$ for nine (ticker, crisis) pairs: \textbf{2008} $\{$AIG, GNW, MGM$\}$, \textbf{2020} $\{$MCD, MGM, AZO$\}$, and \textbf{2022} $\{$SNAP, NFLX, META$\}$. Tickers are chosen to represent sectors that bore the brunt of each crisis. For each pair we use a four-year training window ending six months before the crisis onset, a six-month calibration slice, and a test window that extends six months before through six months after the exogenous crisis end. Crisis onset/end dates are fixed a priori as $\{$2008-09-15 $\rightarrow$ 2009-03-31, 2020-02-20 $\rightarrow$ 2020-04-30, 2022-01-03 $\rightarrow$ 2022-12-31$\}$; deriving them from the returns would confound our adaptation-speed metric.

\subsection{Base predictors}

We use five single-step point predictors. The simple bucket: \textbf{LinAR}, a $5$-lag linear autoregression (sklearn); \textbf{HistBin}, a quantile-bin lookup over lag-window means; \textbf{Trees}, sklearn gradient-boosted regression ($200$ estimators, max\_depth $3$) over lag windows. The deep bucket: \textbf{LSTM}, a one-layer LSTM \citep{hochreiter1997} with a linear head ($5$ lags, $50$ hidden units), and \textbf{Transformer}, a one-layer Transformer encoder \citep{transformers} + linear head ($d_{\text{model}}{=}32$, $4$ heads, dropout $0.1$). Both deep models are trained with mini-batch Adam (batch size $32$, lr $10^{-3}$), $50$ epochs maximum, gradient-norm clipping at $1.0$, with the last $15\%$ of training rows held out chronologically as a validation slice and early stopping on validation MSE (patience $5$). All five predictors return a single scalar forecast for $r_{t+1}$ given the last $5$ returns.

\subsection{Conformal wrappers}

Let $\hat{\mu}_t$ denote the base predictor's forecast at time $t$ and $s_i = |y_i - \hat{\mu}_i|$ the absolute residual on calibration index $i$. With $n$ calibration residuals and miscoverage rate $\alpha = 0.10$, the (finite-sample) conformal quantile is $\hat{q}(\alpha) = s_{(\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil)}$, the order statistic at rank $\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$.

\textbf{Split Conformal.} Fit $\hat{\mu}$ on train, compute residuals on calib, emit $\hat{\mu}_t \pm \hat{q}(\alpha)$ for every test step. No online update.

\textbf{ACI \citep{gibbs2021}.} Same calibration, but maintain an online miscoverage $\alpha_t$: after observing $y_t$, update $\alpha_{t+1} = \alpha_t + \gamma (\alpha - e_t)$ where $e_t = \mathbf{1}[y_t \notin [\hat{\mu}_t \pm \hat{q}(\alpha_t)]]$ and $\gamma = 0.01$, clamped to $[1/(n+1)+\epsilon, 0.5]$ to keep $\hat{q}$ finite.

\textbf{EnbPI \citep{xu2021}.} Train $B = 10$ (deep models) or $25$ (simple) bootstrap predictors, compute leave-one-out residuals over train$+$calib, maintain a sliding residual window of length $|\text{train}|+|\text{calib}|$. At test time, point forecast is the ensemble mean; half-width is $\hat{q}(\alpha)$ of the current window. After each observation, window slides forward by one.

\textbf{AgACI \citep{zaffran2022}.} Run $K = 12$ ACI copies with $\gamma_k$ on $\text{geomspace}(10^{-3}, 10^{-1}, 12)$, sharing one base-model fit and one residual array. At each step produce $K$ symmetric half-widths $q_k$; aggregate lower and upper sides independently via $w_{\text{lo}} \cdot q_k$ and $w_{\text{up}} \cdot q_k$, where $w$ are exponential weights over per-side cumulative pinball loss.

\textbf{TCP \citep{aich2026}.} Rolling window of $w = 252$ days split into $192$ train + $60$ calib. At every test step (or every $k$'th step for expensive models), refit the base predictor on the train slice, compute calibration residuals, emit $\hat{\mu}_t \pm \hat{q}(\alpha)$ from the fresh quantile. No $\alpha$ update --- adaptation comes from the rolling calibration data.

\textbf{TCP-RM.} TCP plus an online Robbins--Monro offset $C_t^{\text{RM}}$ on the conformal threshold: after observing $y_t$, set $C_{t+1}^{\text{RM}} = \max(C_t^{\text{RM}} + \gamma_t (\mathbf{1}[\text{miss}] - \alpha), -\hat{q}_t)$ with $\gamma_t = \gamma_0 / (1 + \lambda t)^\beta$, $\gamma_0 = 0.01$, $\beta = 0.6$. Effective half-width is $\max(\hat{q}_t + C_t^{\text{RM}}, 0)$.

\textbf{Industry baselines.} \textbf{GARCH}(1,1) with Student-$t$ innovations + calibration quantile over $|y - \hat{\mu}| / \sigma_t$; one-step intervals are $\hat{\mu} \pm \hat{q}\, \sigma_{t+1}$. \textbf{FHS} fits GARCH to the returns directly and uses the empirical quantile of standardized residuals scaled by forecasted volatility.

\subsection{Metrics}

For each run we report marginal coverage, conditional coverage stratified by realized-variance quartile (high-vol days = top $25\%$ of rolling 20-day variance, threshold fixed per-asset), interval width, and the Winkler score \citep{gneiting2007}:
\[
W_t = (u_t - l_t) + \frac{2}{\alpha}\max(l_t - y_t, 0) + \frac{2}{\alpha}\max(y_t - u_t, 0),
\]
which jointly penalizes width and miscoverage and is the standard unified probabilistic-forecast metric. Lower is better.

\textbf{Adaptation speed.} The number of trading days from crisis onset until rolling 20-day coverage returns to $\geq 0.85$ for five consecutive days. Onset and end dates are exogenous (see \S 3.1). If this never occurs within the crisis window, we record the full window length ($\sim 137$ days for 2008) and flag the row as unrecovered.


\section{Experiments}

The grid is $180$ runs: $5$ simple/deep models $\times$ $\{$2, 6$\}$ wrappers + $2$ industry baselines, each over $9$ (ticker, crisis) pairs. Simple models (LinAR, HistBin, Trees) receive only Split and ACI wrappers, matching the proposal's framing where these models already produce interpretable native intervals; LSTM and Transformer receive all six CP wrappers. Each run saves an interval array $(l_t, u_t, r_t)$ per trading day; all metrics derive from these arrays plus a fixed volatility mask.

\subsection{Per-regime breakdown and TCP reproducibility}

2008 is the hardest regime for every method --- Lehman was a slow-building credit event with tail days clustering over months --- while 2020 and 2022 are more forgiving. TCP/TCP-RM maintain $0.88$--$0.91$ across all three (\cref{tab:regime}, \cref{app:info}), and this per-regime stability is what drives their low Winkler score in \cref{tab:main}. Our TCP implementation reproduces the qualitative findings of \citet{aich2026}: near-nominal coverage ($0.89$--$0.90$), with intervals systematically wider than ACI. They report TCP coverage $\approx 0.95$ with width $\approx 5.2$ on S\&P 500 daily returns at $\alpha=0.05$; at our $\alpha=0.10$ on per-stock log returns the scales are not directly comparable, but the coverage-width regime is consistent with their characterization.


\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Strengths.} To our knowledge this is the first head-to-head comparison of six CP methods against FHS and GARCH across three distinct crisis regimes, with a deep-predictor extension of TCP. The adaptation-speed metric operationalizes a risk-management concern --- how long miscalibration persists --- largely absent from the CP literature. Conclusions are consistent across regimes: the TCP family dominates, ACI is a safe middle ground, Split CP and industry baselines are unreliable in crises.

\textbf{Weaknesses.} Three tickers per regime is small; a larger basket would tighten per-crisis variance. AIG 2008's GARCH fit converged to non-stationary parameters and was skipped. TCP's coverage comes at $1.5$--$2\times$ the width of ACI at matched base predictors. \textbf{Future work.} TCP's per-step refit is expensive at scale; change-point-gated refitting and portfolio-level TCP are direct follow-ups our codebase supports as drop-in changes.
